There aren't many crafts where the hardest part is waiting for the weather, but sun printing is one of them. And if you live in Queensland, or most of the country this time of year, the weather part is rarely a problem.

Sun printing, properly called cyanotype, makes deep-blue prints with white silhouettes. You lay objects on paper treated with light-sensitive chemicals, leave it in sunlight, rinse it in water, and the shape of whatever you put down appears in white. It's one of the very few crafts that feels like magic and still has a clear explanation.

This is a beginner's guide to doing it at home with a ready-made kit, using leaves and flowers from the garden, and making things you can actually use at Christmas.

## What cyanotype actually is

Cyanotype is an early photographic process from the 1800s. The paper is coated with iron-based light-sensitive chemicals. Where light hits the paper, the coating turns into an insoluble blue compound (Prussian blue). Where something blocks the light, the coating stays soluble and washes away in water, leaving the white of the paper behind.

You don't need to understand more than that to get good results, but it explains the rules:

- Anything that blocks light leaves a white shape.
- Anything that lets some light through leaves a lighter blue.
- The paper must stay in the dark until you're ready, because even ordinary light will slowly expose it.

## What you'll need

- **Ready-coated cyanotype ("sun print") paper.** Craft and art suppliers sell this in packs, often in light-proof bags. I'd strongly suggest beginners buy this rather than mixing the chemicals from scratch.
- **Objects to print.** Leaves, ferns, small flowers, feathers, lace, cut paper shapes or a negative printed on clear film.
- **A flat board.** A piece of cardboard or a clipboard works.
- **Something clear and flat to hold the objects down.** A sheet of glass (with taped edges, or a picture-frame glass) or clear acrylic. This stops the wind and the curling leaf edges from blurring the image.
- **A tray or tub of clean water** for rinsing.
- **Paper towel or a clothesline and pegs** for drying.
- **Gloves** (optional with a pre-coated kit, but I'd wear them for the rinse; follow whatever the kit says).

If you do decide to mix your own coating later, those chemicals need more care. Follow the supplier's safety instructions, wear gloves, and keep everything away from children and pets.

## Which leaves work best

The best prints come from things that are flat, with strong outlines, and thin enough to press down:

- **Ferns and fine foliage.** Delicate, feathery shapes give gorgeous detail.
- **Eucalypt and wattle leaves.** Slim, curved shapes make bold prints. Fallen leaves from your own garden or street are perfect.
- **Flowers pressed first.** If you've [pressed some flowers](/posts/how-to-press-flowers-at-home/), they're flat and print with delicate detail.
- **Grass seed heads and small sprigs.** They give an airy, textural effect.

One practical note: leave native plants in national parks and other protected areas where they are. Use garden clippings, windfall leaves, or things you've grown yourself.

Thick, curved leaves and big flowers are harder. They leave shadows around the edges, which can look soft rather than crisp. That's not a failure, but it's good to know.

## Step by step

### 1. Set up in the shade

Prepare everything indoors or in a shady spot, away from direct sun. The paper is sensitive to light, so lay out your objects and your board before taking the paper out of its bag.

### 2. Arrange the objects

Take one sheet of paper out and put it on the board with the coated side up (it usually looks different from the back; the kit will tell you which). Lay your leaves quickly in the design you want. Overlap them carefully, because anything overlapping blocks light twice and will look the same white.

### 3. Press it flat

Place the glass or acrylic over the top. Light should be able to reach all of the paper that isn't covered. If a leaf is lifting, a slight weight on the glass helps.

### 4. Expose it to the sun

Carry the board into direct sunlight. In strong Queensland sun, exposure can be quick, often a matter of minutes, but the packet instructions will give you the right time for that particular paper, so follow them. Many kits say the paper changes colour as it exposes, from a bright blue to a pale grey-blue. Cloudy days still work because UV passes through cloud, but exposure takes longer.

### 5. Take it back into the shade and rinse

Remove the glass and objects, then rinse the paper in clean water for the time given on the packet, usually a few minutes. You'll see the blue deepen and the exposed areas become sharper. Keep rinsing until the water runs clear.

### 6. Dry flat

Lay the print on paper towel, or peg it to a line. As it dries, the blue gets darker, because the chemicals continue to oxidise. Don't judge it until it's dry. Some kits also suggest a short dip in diluted hydrogen peroxide to speed this up; only do that if your kit says so.

## Common problems and fixes

- **The print is washed out or pale.** Usually under-exposed. Next time, leave it in the sun longer or use a tighter-fitting piece of glass.
- **The edges look blurry.** The object wasn't flat against the paper. Press it harder, or choose flatter leaves.
- **The whole sheet is blotchy or has white streaks.** It may have been exposed before you were ready, or the rinse was uneven. Work in the shade and rinse gently under the water.
- **White spots on the blue.** Often trapped air or water drops on the paper during exposure. Keep the paper dry until it goes in the water.
- **Everything is blue, with no white.** Over-exposed, or the objects didn't block enough light. Try denser leaves or less time.

## What to make with them

The prints themselves are lovely, but here are a few ways to turn them into Christmas presents:

- **Gift tags and cards.** Cut small prints down and glue them to card. A single fern frond on a blue background looks surprisingly polished.
- **Framed prints.** A single large leaf in a simple frame suits a modern home, and costs very little.
- **Wrapping accents.** Stick a small print on brown paper with a bit of twine. It pairs well with the [potato stamp wrapping paper](/posts/potato-stamp-christmas-wrapping-paper/).
- **Bookmarks.** Cut a long strip, print it with a grass or fern, and laminate it or cover with clear contact.
- **Fabric versions.** Pre-treated cyanotype fabric is also sold, so you can try a tea towel or tote bag. Wash it separately and follow the supplier's care advice.

## A weekend plan

Saturday morning: collect leaves and ferns from the garden, press a few of the thicker ones under heavy books for a day. Saturday afternoon: print a first test sheet with a simple design. Sunday: print a few more, dry them, and turn them into tags and cards.

## Closing thought

Sun printing is a craft where the results are a bit unpredictable, and that's the fun. Your first sheet might be too pale, your second is probably better, and by the fourth you'll know exactly what your paper likes. Just keep it out of the light until you're ready, and enjoy watching the white shapes appear when the water hits.
