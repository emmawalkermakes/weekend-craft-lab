Beeswax wraps are one of those projects that look fiddly and turn out to be almost absurdly easy. You need fabric, wax and heat. In an afternoon you can make a stack for your own kitchen, and a set tied with twine makes a practical, useful handmade gift.

There are two common ways to melt the wax into the fabric: in the oven or with an iron. Both work. Below are the steps for each, followed by the problems you're likely to hit and how to fix them.

## What you need

- **100% cotton fabric**, pre-washed and dried. Quilting cotton is ideal: tightly woven, not too thick. Avoid synthetic blends, which don't absorb the wax well.
- **Beeswax**, either pellets or a block you grate. Pellets melt more evenly and save your knuckles.
- **Pinking shears** (optional), to cut zigzag edges that fray less. Ordinary scissors are fine.
- **Baking paper.** Plenty of it.
- **A baking tray** (oven method) or **an iron and ironing board** you don't mind protecting with old towels (iron method).
- **A cheap paintbrush**, for spreading wax.
- **A grater** if you're using a block. It'll be hard to clean, so use an old one you can keep for crafts.

### Optional extras

Many commercial wraps and online recipes add **pine resin** (for extra cling) and a little **jojoba oil** (for flexibility). Wraps made with pure beeswax work, but they're a bit stiffer and less "grippy". If you're making them for the first time, start with beeswax alone; you can experiment later.

## Cutting the fabric

Cut a mix of sizes so the set is genuinely useful:

- **Small (about 20 cm square):** half an avocado, a lemon, the top of a jar.
- **Medium (about 30 cm square):** a bowl, a block of cheese, half a sandwich.
- **Large (about 35–40 cm square):** a sandwich, a loaf end, a big salad bowl.

These are starting points, not rules. Measure the bowls you actually use most.

## Method 1: the oven

Best for making several wraps at once, with even coverage.

1. **Heat the oven low.** Around 80–100°C is plenty. Beeswax melts at roughly 60–65°C, so you don't need much more heat than that. Hotter just risks scorching.
2. **Line the tray with baking paper** and lay one piece of fabric flat on top.
3. **Sprinkle the wax thinly and evenly** across the fabric, all the way to the edges. Less is better than more to start with; you can always add more.
4. **Bake for a few minutes**, watching closely, until all the wax has melted and soaked in.
5. **Brush it out.** Take the tray out and quickly brush the melted wax over any dry patches.
6. **Lift and hang.** The wax is hot, so pick the fabric up by two corners with tongs or very careful fingertips, and wave it gently for a few seconds until the wax sets. Hang it over a clothes airer or the back of a chair to finish cooling.

## Method 2: the iron

Best for one or two wraps, or if you don't want to tie up the oven.

1. **Protect your ironing surface** with an old towel and a large sheet of baking paper.
2. **Lay the fabric on the baking paper** and sprinkle wax evenly on top.
3. **Cover with a second sheet of baking paper.** This is essential. Wax on the soleplate of an iron is a miserable thing to clean.
4. **Iron on a low to medium setting, no steam,** moving slowly from the centre outwards. You'll see the fabric darken as the wax soaks in.
5. **Peel off the top paper** while it's still warm, lift the fabric, and wave it to set.

## Oven versus iron: which is better?

| | Oven | Iron |
|---|---|---|
| Speed for a batch | Faster | Slower |
| Even coverage | Easier | Takes practice |
| Mess | Contained on the tray | Can squeeze out the sides |
| Best for | Making a gift set | Touch-ups and single wraps |

Many people use the oven for the main batch, then the iron to fix any patchy spots.

## Troubleshooting

**There are dry, pale patches.** Add a few more pellets over the patch and reheat. Patchy wraps don't seal well.

**It's stiff and flaky, and wax cracks off.** Too much wax. Put it back on the tray with a fresh, unwaxed piece of fabric underneath and reheat. The extra wax soaks into the new fabric, and you get a bonus wrap.

**It doesn't cling.** Pure beeswax wraps cling less than resin blends. Warming the wrap with your hands as you press it over a bowl helps a lot; the warmth softens the wax so it holds its shape.

**There's wax on my iron.** While it's still warm (and unplugged), wipe the soleplate with a cloth and some baking paper. Go gently.

## Safety notes

- **Never melt wax over high, direct heat.** Wax is flammable. Low oven, low iron and constant attention.
- **Don't leave it unattended** in the oven, even for a minute.
- **Keep tools for craft use.** Wax is hard to remove fully from graters, brushes and trays.

## Using and caring for your wraps

- **Wash in cool water** with a little mild dishwashing liquid, then air dry. Hot water melts the wax.
- **Keep them away from heat:** not in the microwave, not on hot dishes, not in the dishwasher.
- **Don't use them for raw meat or fish.** Because they can only be washed in cool water, most guidance says to stick to other foods.
- **In a Brisbane summer,** wraps can soften in a hot car or on a sunny bench. Store them flat in a drawer or rolled in a jar.
- **Refresh tired wraps** by putting them back in a low oven for a minute or two, adding a few pellets if needed.
- **End of life:** after a lot of use, cut old wraps into strips. Cotton and beeswax are both natural materials; check your local council's guidance on composting.

## Turning them into a gift

A set of three sizes, rolled together and tied with twine or ribbon, looks lovely on its own. Add a small handwritten care card ("cool water, no heat, refresh in a low oven") so the recipient knows how to keep them going. Choose fabrics that suit the person: citrus prints for a keen cook, botanical prints for a gardener, plain colours for a minimalist.

It's the kind of handmade gift that gets used every day, which is the best compliment a handmade gift can get.
