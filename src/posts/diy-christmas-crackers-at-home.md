Shop-bought crackers are fine until you've pulled the same joke three years running and the "gift" turns out to be a plastic whistle that squeaks once. Making your own takes a weekend afternoon, costs less than a fancy box once you reuse tubes and scraps, and lets you put something in the middle that people actually want.

This is a beginner's method using cardboard tubes, crepe or tissue paper, optional snaps, and fillings from around the house. It is written for a Queensland summer kitchen: warm, sometimes humid, and short on spare bench space in December.

A note on voice before we start: this is not a delicate paper-craft tutorial. Expect slightly wonky ends, glue on your fingers, and crackers that look homemade in a good way. That's the point.

## What you need

**For the body**

- Empty cardboard tubes — toilet rolls are the usual size; paper-towel tubes cut in half work too
- Crepe paper, tissue paper or thin wrapping paper, cut into rectangles roughly 30 × 20 cm (adjust if your tubes are longer)
- Ribbon, yarn or twist ties for the ends
- PVA glue or a glue stick, plus double-sided tape if you have it
- Optional: cracker snaps (the thin strips that bang when pulled), sold at craft shops and online — no need for a particular brand

**For decorating**

- Leftover wrapping paper scraps, stickers, washi tape, stamps, or a paint pen
- Paper crowns or hat blanks if you want the full tradition (tissue paper triangles work)

**For filling**

- Small gifts that fit inside a toilet roll (see ideas below)
- Jokes or riddles printed or handwritten on slips of paper
- Optional: a paper crown folded small

**Tools**

- Scissors
- Ruler and pencil
- A chopstick or thin skewer for poking snaps and gifts into place

## Kid-help vs adult-only

| Step | Who |
|---|---|
| Cutting paper rectangles | Older kids with supervision; adults for neat edges |
| Wrapping tubes and tying ends | Great kid job once the snap is in |
| Decorating | Kids of almost any age |
| Inserting snaps | **Adult** — the snap strip is thin and fiddly, and you don't want it tearing early |
| Scissors near the snap | **Adult** |
| Choosing and wrapping tiny gifts | Shared |

If you're making these with primary-school children, pre-cut the paper and have the snaps ready in the tubes before they sit down. They get the fun part without the frustration.

## Step 1: Prep the tubes and paper

Wipe any dust out of the tubes. If a tube is crushed, gently roll it back into shape or swap it — a dented tube makes a lopsided cracker.

Cut your paper into rectangles. The short side should wrap around the tube with a couple of centimetres of overlap. The long side should stick out past each end of the tube by about 7–8 cm so you have enough paper to gather and tie.

Lay the paper face-down on the bench. If you're using crepe paper, notice the grain: it stretches one way. Stretching it lightly across the width can give a softer look, but don't yank it or you'll tear holes.

## Step 2: Add the snap (optional)

Cracker snaps are thin card strips with a tiny bang compound in the middle. You can buy them in packs from craft shops and online craft suppliers. If you can't find them, or you'd rather skip the bang for little kids or pets, leave them out — the cracker still works as a gift wrap.

To fit a snap:

1. Lay the snap along the inside of the tube so each end sticks out past the tube by a few centimetres.
2. Fix it lightly with a dab of glue or a tiny bit of tape in the middle of the tube only — not at the ends, or it won't pull freely.
3. Leave the ends free so diners can grip them when they pull.

Adults should do this step. Once the snap is seated, kids can take over wrapping.

## Step 3: Wrap the tube

1. Run a thin line of glue or a strip of double-sided tape along one long edge of the paper.
2. Place the tube in the middle of the paper, parallel to the short edges.
3. Roll the paper snugly around the tube and press the glued edge down.
4. You should have a paper sleeve with the tube in the centre and empty paper tunnels at each end.

Don't glue the whole wrap — just the seam. You need the ends free to gather.

## Step 4: Tie one end, fill, then tie the other

This order matters. If you fill first and then try to wrap, gifts fall out and snaps shift.

1. Gather the paper at one end, close to the rim of the tube, and tie firmly with ribbon or yarn. A double knot holds better than a bow alone; add a bow on top if you want it pretty.
2. Drop in the joke slip, the folded crown (if using), and the gift. Use a chopstick to nudge things past the middle so the snap isn't bent.
3. Gather and tie the other end the same way.
4. Fluff the paper ends with your fingers so they look like classic cracker frills. Crepe paper frills nicely; thicker wrapping paper may need a few snips along the edge to help it flare.

If a snap end got tucked inside the tie, gently fish it out with the chopstick before you finish the knot.

## Step 5: Decorate

Now they look like crackers instead of wrapped toilet rolls.

Ideas that work in a humid house (minimal wet glue):

- Wrap a band of leftover wrapping paper around the middle and fix with double-sided tape
- Stick on a name tag so people know whose cracker is whose at the table
- Washi tape stripes
- A paint-pen doodle (stars, gum leaves, a simple bauble) — let it dry fully before stacking
- A sprig of fake greenery or a small dried flower tucked under the ribbon (real greenery wilts fast in Brisbane heat)

Avoid thick layers of PVA on the outside if the forecast is sticky; glue stays tacky longer in humid weather and crackers will stick together in a box.

## Filling ideas from around the house

Keep gifts small enough to slide into a toilet roll without forcing. Under common household items:

- A few quality chocolates or wrapped sweets (check dietary needs at the table)
- Hair clips, a lip balm, or a nice biro
- Mini seed packets
- A pair of cheerful socks rolled tight (thin ones)
- Puzzle erasers, a tiny notebook, or a coil of nice string for the maker in the family
- A tea bag sampler and a sugar stick
- Homemade: a folded origami star, a handmade joke booklet, a coupon for "I'll do the dishes on Boxing Day"

Skip anything sharp, messy, or that melts in a hot car on the way to someone else's place. Soft plastics that smell strongly can also be unpleasant when the cracker sits in a warm room for a week.

## How many to make, and a simple production line

For a table of eight, make eight plus one spare. Work in batches:

1. Cut all paper first
2. Seat all snaps
3. Wrap all tubes
4. Tie, fill and finish one at a time with the decorations laid out nearby

Music on, windows open, and accept that the first one will look slightly stranger than the fifth. That's normal.

## Storing crackers in humid Queensland weather

This is where shop tutorials written for a cold, dry winter Christmas fall apart.

- **Store in a cool, dry cupboard**, not the garage and not next to the oven.
- Use a **cardboard box with a lid**, lined with baking paper so decorative glue doesn't stick to neighbours.
- Add a **small sachet of silica gel** if you have one from shoe boxes or vitamin bottles — it helps with damp air.
- Don't seal them in plastic long-term; trapped moisture makes paper go soft and ink smudge.
- Make them **within a week or two of Christmas** if you can. Crepe paper softens and colours can migrate if they sit in humidity for a month.
- If a snap has been sitting in damp air, it may fail to bang. That's disappointing but not dangerous — treat it as a quiet cracker and move on.

On Christmas Day, keep the box indoors until the meal. Leaving crackers in a hot car while you do last-minute shopping can warp the paper and soften chocolate fillings.

## Pulling them without chaos

Agree a plan before everyone yanks at once:

- Cross-arms tradition, or pull with the person next to you
- Read the joke out loud (even the bad ones)
- Crowns on for the main course — non-negotiable in some households

If a cracker tears instead of snapping, the tie was probably too close to the gift or the snap wasn't free. Pull straight out, not up.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely cause | Fix next time |
|---|---|---|
| Paper tears when tying | Paper too thin or pulled too hard | Double-layer tissue, or use crepe |
| Snap doesn't bang | Snap stuck under the tie, or damp storage | Free the ends before tying; store dry |
| Gift won't fit | Gift too big | Smaller fillings, or use a paper-towel tube cut shorter |
| Crackers stick together in the box | Glue still tacky | Longer dry time; baking paper between layers |
| Ends look sparse | Not enough overhang | Cut longer paper rectangles |

## Why bother

Homemade crackers are one of the few Christmas crafts that earn their keep at the actual meal. They're conversation, decoration and a gift in one, and you control what ends up on the table — no mystery plastic, no duplicate jokes if you write your own.

Set aside a Saturday morning in late November or early December. By lunchtime you'll have a box of slightly imperfect, completely personal crackers ready for the hottest day of the year.
