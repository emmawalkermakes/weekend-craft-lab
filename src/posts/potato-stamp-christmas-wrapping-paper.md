Every year I tell myself I'll buy wrapping paper early, and every year I end up in a shop on the 23rd of December staring at the last roll of something with cartoon reindeer on it. A few years ago I tried the opposite approach: spend one slow Sunday in November stamping my own paper using nothing fancier than potatoes. It cost very little, the results looked deliberately handmade rather than accidentally cheap, and I had enough paper left over for birthdays in the new year.

This is the whole process, including the things that went wrong the first time.

## What you need

- **Plain paper to stamp on.** Kraft paper, butcher's paper or brown packing paper all work. Smooth, plain, light-coloured paper shows paint best. Recycled paper from a parcel works too if you iron out the creases with a cool iron.
- **Potatoes.** Large, firm ones with a flat side are easiest to carve. Waxy or all-purpose varieties tend to hold detail better than very floury ones, but use whatever is in the pantry.
- **Craft or acrylic paint.** Water-based, non-toxic paint is fine. A small amount of each colour goes a long way.
- **A sharp knife and a small cutting board.** A butter knife is too blunt. If children are helping, you do the cutting and they do the stamping.
- **Cookie cutters (optional).** Handy for pressing a shape into the potato so you can carve around it.
- **Paper towels, a plate or foam tray for paint, and old newspaper** to protect the table.
- **A ruler and pencil** if you like neat repeats. I usually don't, and that's part of the charm.

## Step 1: Cut and dry your potato

Cut the potato in half crossways, so you have a large flat face. Pat that face firmly with a paper towel. This matters more than you'd think: a wet potato makes a blotchy print because the paint slides on the moisture instead of sticking. Leave the cut halves face-down on a paper towel for about ten minutes while you set up. They'll sweat a bit more, so blot them again before carving.

## Step 2: Carve a simple shape

Choose shapes with bold outlines. Fine detail doesn't survive, because potato flesh is soft and paint spreads a little. Good first designs:

- A **star** (or a simple five-point star you've drawn with pencil on the potato).
- A **small tree**: a triangle with a tiny rectangle for the trunk.
- A **circle** you can use as a bauble, a berry or a snowball.
- A **diamond or leaf shape**, which looks lovely repeated in rows.
- A **gum-leaf shape**, if you want something that suits an Australian summer Christmas.

To carve, press a cookie cutter about a centimetre into the potato, or draw your shape on the surface with a pencil. Then cut straight down around the outline, and slice horizontally from the side so the background drops away and your shape stands raised, a bit like a rubber stamp. Aim for a raised area about a centimetre high. Trim the edges clean, then blot the stamp again.

Make a few stamps before you start. Having separate stamps for each colour saves washing and waiting.

## Step 3: Test before you commit

Squeeze a small blob of paint on a plate and spread it into a thin, even layer with the back of a spoon or a piece of cardboard. Thin is the key word. A thick coat of paint turns into a blob on the paper and fills in your carving.

Press the stamp into the paint, lift, and try it on a scrap of the same paper. You're looking for crisp edges and even coverage. If the print is blotchy, the potato is probably still wet or the paint is too thick. If it's patchy and pale, press harder or reload with a bit more paint. It usually takes three or four test stamps before things settle, and the first print on any loading is the darkest.

## Step 4: Stamp the paper

Lay out a sheet of paper on the newspaper. Decide on your pattern: a loose scatter, neat rows, or a grid. If you want neat rows, make light pencil dots with a ruler first. Then:

1. Load the stamp with paint.
2. Press down firmly and evenly, without rocking.
3. Lift straight up.
4. Reload every one to two stamps. The prints will naturally fade slightly, and I actually like that variation.

If you want two colours, let the first colour dry before adding the second, especially if they overlap, or the colours will smear into a muddy brown. Small details like dots between the stars can be added with a pencil eraser dipped in paint, which makes a neat round stamp for free.

## Step 5: Dry it flat, and be patient in humid weather

Acrylic paint on thin paper dries quickly in dry weather, but in a humid week in south-east Queensland, or anywhere coastal during summer, it can feel tacky for much longer. Lay the sheets flat and don't stack them until the paint is properly dry to the touch. A fan pointed gently at the sheets helps. Stacking them early is the fastest way to end up with Christmas trees printed on the back of the next sheet.

If the paper curls as it dries, press it under a few heavy books overnight once the paint has dried. Curling comes from moisture in the paint and is easy to flatten out.

## Matching gift tags

Use up the offcuts. Cut rectangles of cardboard, from a cereal box if you like, punch a hole in one corner, and stamp one of your shapes on the front. Write the name in pen on the back or on a blank space on the front. Thread a bit of string or kitchen twine through the hole. Cereal box cardboard is stiff enough to stand up to being tied on, and if you stamp the inside of the box (the plain grey side) you get a nice plain-tone tag.

If you've got a few names to get through, make the tags in one batch and write them at the end. Handwriting after the paint has dried looks neater than trying to write on a tacky surface.

## What went wrong the first time

- **I carved too deep and too fine.** My first stamp was a detailed snowflake. It turned into a vaguely round blob. Simple wins.
- **I used too much paint.** The prints looked like thick stickers and took forever to dry.
- **I stamped over the edge of the sheet** and printed the table. Newspaper under everything solved that.
- **I didn't test on scrap.** The first stamp on the real sheet always came out heavy. Test first.
- **I stored the potato stamps overnight.** They went soft and brown. They're a single-session tool; carve, use, bin (or compost).

## Making it last past this year

A sheet of home-stamped paper looks nice, but you can also stamp:

- Plain **paper bags** from the supermarket, which become reusable gift bags.
- **Tissue paper** for tucking into gift boxes. Use less paint, as it's thin.
- **Plain calico or cotton bags**, with fabric paint instead. Follow the paint's instructions about heat setting.
- **Christmas cards** made from folded cardstock, using one of the stamps as the front-of-card design.

One practical note: wrapping paper stamped with water-based paint is fine for most gifts, but if a present is going to be handled in the heat, say in a car on a hot day, give the paint a good day or two to cure before wrapping, or it can stick to the gift box.

## Is it worth it?

If you're after quick and neat, a roll of bought paper is quicker. If you enjoy a pottering afternoon with the radio on, and you like the idea of every present under the tree looking handmade, it's a good one. It's also one of the few craft projects where the mistakes, the slightly wonky stars and uneven dots, make the finished thing look better, not worse.

Make a batch this weekend, let it dry properly, roll it up, and you'll be ahead for the rest of the season.
