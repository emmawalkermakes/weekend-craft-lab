Northern-hemisphere Christmas photos are full of pine, fir and snow. That look is beautiful, and it has almost nothing to do with December in Brisbane. The days are long and sticky, and the last thing I want on the mantel is something that smells like a forest in winter.

A dried citrus and eucalyptus garland suits this climate much better. Oranges — or lemons and limes if that's what you have — dry into translucent discs. Eucalyptus, which many of us already have in the garden, brings a clean scent that feels like an Australian summer rather than a European one. Slice and dry on Saturday, string on Sunday. Slices will not all look identical. Some will brown more than others. That's fine.

## Choosing the fruit

Oranges are the usual choice, and they're what I reach for first. They slice into generous rounds and, once dry, hold a warm colour that reads as Christmas without needing red ribbon everywhere.

Lemons and limes work too if you have a tree that's producing, or fruit you won't eat in time. They dry smaller and paler — more green-gold than amber — and look lovely mixed with orange. I don't have a scientific ranking of which citrus performs best. Use what you have, as long as it feels firm and looks fresh. Soft or mouldy fruit is not worth drying.

A few medium oranges give you plenty of slices. I dry more than I think I need, because some will scorch or stay a bit sticky. Wash the fruit and dry the skins before you cut; dirt on the peel will be there for the life of the decoration.

## Slicing evenly, then patting dry

Aim for even rounds, roughly the same thickness, so they dry at a similar pace. I go for about half a centimetre — thin enough to dry through, thick enough that they don't shatter when you thread them. Uneven slices mean the thin ones are ready while the thick ones are still wet in the middle.

A sharp knife and a steady board matter more than any special tool. Cut across the fruit, not stem-to-tip, so you get the star pattern of the segments.

Lay the slices in a single layer on a clean tea towel or paper towel and pat both sides. Take off the surface juice; don't press them into pulp. Wet slices take longer and are more likely to steam and go dull rather than translucent. Pick off loose seeds. If a slice falls apart, set it aside — it won't string neatly.

## Drying: a low oven, or patience on a rack

I've used two approaches at home: a low oven when I want results the same day, and air-drying when the weather is dry enough.

**A low oven** is what I use most often, because a Queensland kitchen is not always a dry place. A commonly used home method is a very low oven — people often work somewhere around 90–120°C — with the slices in a single layer on a baking tray lined with baking paper. I leave the door slightly ajar with a wooden spoon if my oven will hold a low temperature, so moisture can escape rather than circulating as steam. Ovens vary a lot, including mine from one month to the next, so treat any temperature as a starting point rather than a lab specification.

Check regularly. You're looking for slices that feel dry and leathery, not sticky, and that lift cleanly off the paper. The centres should not look wet. Some edges will curl; that's normal. If a slice darkens quickly or smells toasted, it's too hot — drop the temperature or move the tray down.

How long this takes depends on your oven, the thickness of the slices, and how juicy the fruit was. I start checking early and flip them once so both sides get air. Go slowly rather than rushing and browning the batch. A slice that felt dry in a warm oven can still feel soft as it cools on a rack; if so, it can go back in. Ready slices feel light and dry through, and slightly translucent against the window.

**Air-drying** works somewhere warm, airy and out of harsh sun — a rack with good airflow, slices not touching. In a humid week this can take many days, and slices may soften or spot before they dry. If the washing won't dry on the line, use the oven. Let finished slices sit out a while either way; leftover moisture shows up as softness by the next morning.

## Eucalyptus: garden clippings are enough

In Queensland, eucalyptus in the garden is ordinary rather than exotic. If you have a tree — or a kind neighbour who does — a few small branches are plenty. You do not need a particular species. Choose stems flexible enough to curve into a garland, with leaves that look healthy rather than crispy and brown. Shake them outside to dislodge spiders or grit, rinse them if they're dusty, then let them dry off.

Florists and greengrocers often sell bunches as well, fresh or already dried. Fresh clippings wilt a little on the garland, which I quite like — the leaves fold and the colour deepens. Already-dried stems hold their shape but can be more brittle. Don't strip a public tree or take greenery from bushland. Trim stems into lengths you can handle; I use pieces about as long as my forearm, plus a few smaller sprigs for gaps.

## Stringing the garland

I use natural twine or strong cotton string. Fishing line is nearly invisible if you want the fruit and leaves to look as if they're floating; twine looks more handmade, which suits this project. Make a hole in each slice with a skewer or thick needle. Work gently near the rind rather than through the very centre if the middle is fragile — a hole a little off-centre is stronger than one that tears the pith.

A simple pattern: tie a loop at one end, thread a citrus slice, add a small bunch of eucalyptus (tying or twisting the stems so they sit rather than slide), and repeat, varying the angle of the slices so it doesn't look like a kebab. Hang a length over a chair now and then — garlands look different hanging than they do on the table. If a slice cracks, don't force it.

A short length for a mantel or table is easier than a doorway swag, and you can join two short ones. Leave a loop at both ends.

## Hanging it somewhere sensible

Dried plant material can be flammable. This is ordinary caution, not a scare: keep the garland away from open flames, candles, the barbecue, the stovetop, and hot light bulbs. Fairy lights that stay cool to the touch are a different thing from an old incandescent bulb that gets hot. If you wouldn't leave a bunch of dry leaves next to it, don't hang the garland there either.

I hang mine along a mantel, over a mirror, or on a wall hook well clear of the kitchen hob. If you want it on the table as a runner, keep candles on a separate surface. Use a hook that can take a little weight — fresh eucalyptus plus dried fruit is heavier than it looks.

## Humidity, storage, and how long it lasts

In a Queensland summer, dried citrus can soften again if the air is very humid. Hang the garland somewhere airy — a living room or hallway rather than a steamy kitchen if you can help it — and don't drape it over the kettle.

Spare slices should go into an airtight jar once they're fully dry. I keep the jar in a cupboard, not on a sunny windowsill. If a stored slice later feels tacky, it wasn't quite dry enough; you can try another gentle session in a low oven.

As a seasonal decoration, a garland like this generally sees you through the season if you keep it dry. I don't treat it as a permanent heirloom. By late January the eucalyptus is usually brittle and the citrus a little faded, which is a natural point to take it down. If you notice any mould, that's the end of that piece.
