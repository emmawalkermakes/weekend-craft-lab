These are the stars you see hanging in shop windows and strung across windows at Christmas: crisp, eight-pointed and surprisingly sculptural for something made from two squares of paper. Once you've folded one, you'll make ten. They're good for a quiet evening, cheap enough to experiment with, and simple enough for older kids to help.

## What you need

- **Two squares of paper per star**, the same size. 15 cm squares make a star about the size of your hand; 30 cm squares make a big statement piece for a window.
- **Scissors**
- **Glue stick** or double-sided tape
- **A ruler and pencil** (optional, but helpful for the first one)
- **String, ribbon or fishing line** for hanging

### Which paper works best

- **Medium-weight paper** (thicker than printer paper, thinner than card) holds crisp points without being hard to fold. Scrapbooking paper is ideal.
- **Kraft paper** gives a lovely rustic look and matches brown-paper wrapping.
- **Printer paper** works for practice, but the points can droop.
- **Old book pages, maps or sheet music** look great, but you'll need to trim them square.
- **Thick card** is hard to fold neatly. Skip it.

If your paper has a pattern only on one side, the patterned side should face up when you start folding.

Each square becomes one half of the star: a four-pointed, raised shape. Two halves glued back to back make the full eight-pointed star.

### 1. Fold the creases

Take one square, patterned side up.

- Fold it in half edge to edge, crease firmly, and unfold.
- Fold it in half the other way, crease, and unfold.
- Turn the paper over so the plain side faces up. Fold it in half diagonally, corner to corner, crease, and unfold. Repeat along the other diagonal.

You now have eight creases radiating out from the centre, like the spokes of a wheel: four straight (edge-to-edge) and four diagonal.

### 2. Cut along the straight creases

Along each of the four **straight** creases, cut from the edge towards the centre. Stop roughly a third of the way short of the centre. If your square is 15 cm, that means cutting about 5 cm in from each edge.

Don't cut along the diagonals.

**The most common mistake:** cutting too far. If you cut close to the centre, the star will be floppy and may fall apart. Mark a little pencil dot where to stop on your first star.

### 3. Shape the points

Turn the paper back so the patterned side faces up. Each cut now has a flap of paper on either side of it.

- Check the diagonal creases pop **up** like a mountain ridge, and the straight creases sit **down** like a valley. If not, gently press them into shape.
- At one cut, slide the flap on one side over the flap on the other side, so they overlap and form a triangle. As they overlap, the diagonal crease next to it lifts into a raised, three-dimensional point.
- Glue the overlapping flaps together and hold for a few seconds.

Repeat at all four cuts, overlapping in the same direction each time. You now have a four-pointed, raised half-star.

### 4. Make the second half

Repeat steps 1 to 3 with the second square.

### 5. Join the halves

Put a small dab of glue on the flat underside of each point of one half. Place the second half on top, back to back, rotated so its points sit between the points of the first half. That rotation is what gives you eight points instead of four.

Before the glue sets, slip a loop of string or ribbon between the two halves at one point if you plan to hang the star.

## Tips for neat stars

- **Crease hard.** Run a fingernail or the back of a spoon along every fold. Sharp creases make sharp points.
- **Overlap evenly.** If one pair of flaps overlaps more than the others, that point will look slightly different. Some unevenness is charming; lots looks messy.
- **Let the glue dry** before hanging, especially in humid weather when glue sticks can take longer to grab.
- **Make a few in different sizes.** A cluster of three sizes on one string looks more deliberate than a row of matching stars.

- **Window garland.** Thread several small stars onto a long string at different heights and hang them across a window. They look especially good with daylight behind them.
- **Tree topper.** A large kraft star makes a simple topper. Attach it with a bit of wire or a pipe cleaner wrapped around the top branch.
- **Gift toppers.** A small star glued to a parcel instead of a bow. Useful if you've wrapped presents in plain paper.
- **Table scatter.** A handful of small, flat-backed stars down the middle of the Christmas lunch table. Weigh down any that might blow away if you're eating outside under a fan.
- **Mobile.** Hang stars from a stick or embroidery hoop at different lengths.

## Making them with kids

Older primary kids can usually manage all the folding and gluing. Younger kids can do the creasing and gluing while an adult does the cutting. Precut the squares beforehand, mark the stopping point for each cut, and keep a few spare squares on hand for the inevitable "I cut too far".

## Storing them

Because the points are hollow, these stars can crush in a box with heavy decorations. Store them in a shoebox on top of everything else, or nest stars of different sizes on a string and hang them in a wardrobe until next year.
