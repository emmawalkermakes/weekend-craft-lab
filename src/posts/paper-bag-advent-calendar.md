Advent calendars start on 1 December, which makes the second half of November the sweet spot for making one. Leave it until the last weekend and you'll be numbering bags at eleven o'clock on a Sunday night with a pen that's running out.

A paper bag calendar is about the most forgiving version there is. There's no carpentry, no sewing and nothing to glue into a box. You number 24 small bags, hang them on a string, and fill them. If one tears, you swap it for a fresh bag and nobody notices. Packed flat, most of the calendar can be reused next year.

This guide walks through it in order: gather, number, decorate, hang, then fill. Set aside an afternoon for the making, and a separate, shorter session for the filling.

## Step 1: Gather your materials

You need surprisingly little:

- **24 small paper bags.** Brown kraft lunch bags or small paper party bags both work. Flat-bottomed bags stand up and hold more; flat envelope-style bags are slimmer and hang more neatly. Buy a few spares.
- **Numbers.** Stickers, a stamp set, a stencil, or just a good marker. More on this below.
- **Something to hang them from.** Garden twine, baker's twine, ribbon or thin rope, plus mini pegs or bulldog clips. A dowel or a bare branch works if you'd rather hang them from a rod.
- **Closures.** Washi tape, mini pegs, stickers or a single hole punched through the folded top and tied with string.
- **Optional decoration.** Gum leaves or a sprig of rosemary, a stamp, leftover wrapping paper, a white paint pen.

A word on bag size: the smaller the bag, the easier it is to fill. Big bags look generous on the wall and then sit there accusingly half-empty. Lunch-bag size folded down by a third is plenty.

## Step 2: Decide on a numbering style

The numbers are what makes it read as an advent calendar rather than a row of lunch bags, so it's worth choosing a style before you start. A few options, from easiest to most fiddly:

1. **Number stickers.** Quick and tidy, and fine for a first go.
2. **A marker or paint pen.** Pencil the number in lightly first, then go over it. Write larger than feels natural; numbers look smaller once the bags are hanging.
3. **Stamps.** A set of number stamps and an ink pad gives a neat, consistent look. Test the ink on a spare bag first, because some inks bleed into kraft paper.
4. **Stencils.** Hold the stencil flat with a bit of low-tack tape and dab paint on with a sponge rather than brushing, so it doesn't creep under the edges.

If you've made [potato stamp wrapping paper](/posts/potato-stamp-christmas-wrapping-paper/), a small star or tree stamp from the same session makes a nice motif next to each number.

## Step 3: Number and decorate

Work in batches rather than finishing one bag at a time. Lay out all 24, fold the tops down to the height you want, and crease them so you know where the visible face will be. Then number every bag. Then add decoration to every bag.

Batching sounds fussy, but it's faster and the set looks more consistent, because your hand settles into a rhythm. It also means that if you decide halfway through that the numbers are too small, you've only lost one round of work.

Leave paint and ink to dry properly before stacking. On a humid day in Queensland, paint pen and stamp ink can stay tacky for longer than you'd expect. Spread the bags out on the table or along the floor of a room with the fan on.

Decoration is optional. A single gum leaf held on with washi tape, a stamped star, or a strip of leftover wrapping paper across the fold all look good. Keep it simple, because you're doing it 24 times.

## Step 4: Choose where it will hang

Pick the spot before you string anything, because it decides how long the line needs to be. Measure it with a tape measure or a length of string.

Some good spots:

- **Along a wall** in a long line or a zigzag of two or three rows.
- **Down the side of a bookcase or doorframe** in a vertical drop.
- **From a dowel or bare branch** hung with a loop of twine, with bags clipped at different heights.
- **On a clothes airer or a ladder** in a corner, if you'd rather not put hooks in the wall.

Avoid hanging it in direct afternoon sun, near the stove, or above a heater. Sun fades kraft paper and ink, and warm spots are a problem for any fillers that melt.

## Step 5: String it up

Tie your string to one fixing point, leave some slack, and tie the other end. Removable adhesive hooks are handy for walls you don't want to drill. Check the weight rating on the packet, and remember that 24 filled bags weigh more than 24 empty ones.

Then clip the bags on in order, 1 to 24. Hang them all before filling anything, because you'll want to see the spacing. If the line sags too much in the middle, add a third hook or switch to two shorter rows.

Some people like to hang the numbers out of order so finding each day's bag is a small game. It's a lovely idea for older kids and a recipe for frustration with very young ones. You know your household.

## Step 6: Plan the fillers for an Australian December

This is where an Aussie advent calendar differs from the ones in northern-hemisphere craft books. December here is hot, and a string of paper bags on a wall is not a fridge.

**Chocolate is risky in a Queensland December.** It will soften and can melt in a warm room, and a melted bag of chocolate on day 19 is a sad thing. If you want chocolate in the mix, keep those portions in the fridge and add them to the bag each morning, or use a slip of paper that says "chocolate is in the fridge".

Non-food ideas that hold up in the heat:

- **Notes with a small activity.** "Make paper snowflakes", "Watch a Christmas movie tonight", "Write a card to someone you haven't seen this year", "Go for an ice cream".
- **Small craft supplies.** A sheet of stickers, a mini roll of washi tape, a pack of coloured pencils split across a few days.
- **Decorations.** One ornament a day for a small tree, and by 24 December it's decorated.
- **Puzzle pieces.** Split a small jigsaw across the bags and finish it on Christmas Eve.
- **Garden or outdoor bits.** A seed packet, sidewalk chalk, a water pistol for a hot afternoon.
- **For adults:** a nice tea bag, a hand-written recipe card, a lottery ticket, a voucher for "I'll do the dishes".

Mix the big and the small. Not every day needs a present. A note saying "Tonight we're having a picnic dinner on the lawn" is often better received than another small plastic thing.

## Step 7: Fill it without stress

Filling 24 bags at once is the part that tends to stall, so break it up:

1. **Write a list of 24 numbered lines** before you shop or gather anything. Fill in the easy ones first.
2. **Put the bigger or more exciting items on weekends** and around the end of the school term, when there's time to enjoy them.
3. **Keep activity notes realistic.** A busy weeknight is not the night for "bake gingerbread".
4. **Fill a week at a time.** If you fill days 1–7 now and the rest by the end of the first week of December, you'll avoid the last-minute scramble.
5. **Seal the top** with your chosen closure so little hands can't peek.

Keep a couple of spare activity notes in your pocket for the day something goes wrong, like a bag torn open early or a planned outing rained off.

## Step 8: Pack it away for next year

On Boxing Day, or whenever the decorations come down, unclip the bags, flatten the ones that survived, and store them in a large envelope with the string wound around a piece of cardboard. Pop your list of fillers in the envelope too. Next November you'll be glad of a head start, and you only need to make replacements for the bags that didn't make it.
