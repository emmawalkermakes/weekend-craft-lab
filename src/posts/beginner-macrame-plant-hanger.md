Macramé looks intricate, but most beginner plant hangers use only two knots. If you can tie a shoelace, you can learn both in ten minutes. By the end of an afternoon you'll have a hanger that can hold a small pot of trailing plants — and probably a plan for the next three.

This guide walks through a simple, classic design: a gathered top, a short section of knots, an open "net" that cradles the pot, and a tassel at the bottom.

## What you need

- **Macramé cord, 3–5 mm thick.** Cotton cord is soft and easy for beginners. Single-twist cord gives a fluffy fringe; three-ply rope is more structured. Either works.
- **A wooden or metal ring**, about 5 cm across, for the top.
- **Scissors and a tape measure.**
- **Somewhere to hang your work** while you knot: a hook, a door handle, a clothes rack, or a hook screwed into the underside of a shelf.
- **A pot** to test-fit as you go. A pot about 12–15 cm across suits this design.

## Cutting your cord

For a hanger that sits a comfortable length below a hook, cut:

- **8 cords, each about 3 m long.** These will be folded in half, so you'll end up with 16 working strands of about 1.5 m.
- **2 shorter cords, each about 1 m long,** for the two gathering knots.

These lengths give you some spare. Macramé eats cord faster than you'd expect, and it's very hard to add more later. If you want a longer hanger, add length now.

## Learn the two knots first

Practise both on a scrap of cord before you start the real thing.

### The square knot

A square knot uses four strands. The two outer strands do the tying (the "working cords"). The two middle strands just hang straight (the "filler cords").

1. Take the **left** working cord and cross it over the two filler cords, so it makes a shape like the number 4.
2. Take the **right** working cord, lay it over the tail of the left cord, pass it **under** the fillers, and bring it up through the gap on the left side.
3. Pull both working cords to tighten. That's half a square knot.
4. Now do the mirror image: take the **right** working cord across the fillers first, making a backwards 4. Take the left cord over its tail, under the fillers and up through the gap on the right.
5. Tighten. That's one full square knot. It should lie flat.

**Check:** if your knots start twisting into a spiral, you've repeated the same side twice instead of alternating. (That spiral is actually a decorative knot called a half-knot spiral, so it's not wrong, just different.)

### The gathering knot

Also called a wrapping knot, this neatly binds a bundle of cords together.

1. Lay a short cord along the bundle, folded into a long U shape, with the loop pointing down and a short tail sticking up above.
2. Take the long end and wrap it tightly around the bundle (and the U) from top to bottom, several times. Keep the wraps neat and close together.
3. Thread the end of the long cord through the loop at the bottom of the U.
4. Pull the short tail at the top firmly. This drags the loop, and the end inside it, up under the wraps, locking it in place.
5. Trim both ends close to the wraps.

## Making the hanger

### Step 1: The top

Thread all 8 long cords through the ring and fold them in half, so the ring sits in the middle and you have 16 strands hanging down. Even up the ends.

Hang the ring on your hook. Just below the ring, tie a **gathering knot** around all 16 strands to hold them together. Wrap it to a height of about 2–3 cm for a tidy look.

### Step 2: The arms

Divide the 16 strands into **4 groups of 4**.

On each group, leave a gap of about 25–30 cm below the gathering knot (this becomes the upper "arm"), then tie a short run of square knots, say 4 to 6. Try to start all four groups at the same height. Measuring from the gathering knot helps.

### Step 3: The net

This is the part that creates the basket shape.

- Move about 10 cm down from the end of your square knots.
- Take **two strands from one group** and **two strands from the neighbouring group**, and tie one square knot with these four.
- Work your way around until all four new groups are tied. The last one joins the final group back to the first, closing the circle.

Repeat once more, another 8–10 cm lower, swapping groups again. You'll see a diamond-shaped net appear.

**Test-fit the pot now.** Sit it in the net. If it slips through, your net knots are too far apart; untie and move them closer.

### Step 4: The bottom

Gather all 16 strands together a few centimetres below the last row of knots, where the bottom of the pot will sit, and tie your second **gathering knot**.

### Step 5: The tassel

Trim the ends to an even length. A tassel of about 15–25 cm looks balanced. If you used single-twist cotton, you can comb the ends out with a fine-toothed comb for a soft fringe.

## Common mistakes

- **Running out of cord.** Cut longer than you think. Spare cord can become a keyring.
- **Uneven arms.** Measure each arm from the same point before you tie the first square knot.
- **Knots too loose or too tight.** Aim for firm and consistent. It matters more that every knot is similar than that they're especially tight.
- **A net that's too open.** The pot needs to be held from underneath. Tighten the spacing if in doubt.

## Choosing a plant (and hanging it safely)

Trailing plants look best: devil's ivy (pothos), string of pearls, a spider plant, or a small fern. Consider how heavy the pot will be once it's watered, and use a pot with a saucer, or a plastic inner pot, so drips don't land on the floor.

Hang it from a sturdy hook that's properly fixed into a ceiling joist, a beam, or a strong bracket. A wet pot of soil is heavier than it looks.

## As a gift

A macramé hanger makes a great handmade gift with or without a plant. Roll it neatly, tie it with a strip of spare cord, and attach a tag with the plant's watering needs if you include one. For the keen gardener, add a cutting in a small pot; for a new homeowner, add a hook.

Once you've made one, try a variation: add a few wooden beads between knots, or try the spiral knot for the arms. Two knots really do go a long way.
