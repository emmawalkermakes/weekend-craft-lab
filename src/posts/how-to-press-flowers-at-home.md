Late spring is my favourite time for pressing flowers. Gardens are full, verges are flowering, and the weather in most of Australia is still dry enough that petals don't turn mouldy before they flatten. If you've ever wanted to make your own pressed-flower cards, bookmarks or framed pieces, this is the weekend project to start now, so you have a stash ready for Christmas cards and gift tags.

I've pressed flowers on and off for years, mostly badly at first. This post covers what I wish someone had told me: which flowers press well, three methods from slowest to fastest, and the small mistakes that ruin a batch.

## Start with the right flowers

Not every flower wants to be flat. The easiest ones are naturally thin with a single layer of petals. Thick, fleshy or multi-layered flowers, like roses and camellias, hold a lot of water and tend to brown or go mouldy unless you take them apart first.

Good beginner choices:

- **Pansies and violas.** The classic. They press flat, keep their colour well and look lovely on cards.
- **Cosmos and daisies.** Simple shapes, easy to handle.
- **Ferns and small leaves.** Great fillers, and very forgiving.
- **Lavender and rosemary sprigs.** They keep a little scent, too.
- **Small native flowers.** Plenty of Australian natives press nicely; experiment with whatever grows in your own garden.

A note on picking: only take flowers from your own garden, or ask first. Many native plants in bushland and national parks are protected, so leave those where they are.

## Pick at the right time of day

Pick mid-morning, after the dew has dried but before the afternoon heat makes everything wilt. Choose flowers that have just opened, not ones that are already dropping petals. Press them as soon as you can, ideally within an hour or two. A flower that has started to droop will press with creases.

Gently blot off any moisture with a paper towel before pressing. Water is the enemy here.

## Method 1: The heavy book (slowest, simplest)

This is how most of us started, and it still works.

1. Open a large, heavy book towards the back, so there's plenty of weight on top.
2. Lay a sheet of baking paper or plain printer paper on the page. Never put flowers straight onto the book, because the moisture can stain or wrinkle the pages.
3. Arrange the flowers face down, not touching each other.
4. Cover with a second sheet of paper and close the book.
5. Stack more books on top.
6. Leave it for two to three weeks. After the first few days, swap the paper sheets for fresh, dry ones to draw out more moisture.

Pros: free, no equipment. Cons: slow, and it's easy to forget which book you used. I write a note on a sticky label on the spine now.

## Method 2: A simple DIY flower press

If you plan to press regularly, a basic press is worth an afternoon of making.

You'll need two pieces of plywood or MDF the same size (around 20 to 25 cm square is plenty), four long bolts with wing nuts, some cardboard and some absorbent paper. Drill a hole in each corner of both boards, lined up. Then build a stack from the bottom up: board, cardboard, paper, flowers, paper, cardboard, and repeat for more layers. Top it with the second board, push the bolts through and tighten the wing nuts evenly.

The cardboard lets air move between layers, which helps the flowers dry rather than steam. Tighten the nuts a little more each day for the first few days. Most flowers are ready in one to three weeks.

If woodwork isn't your thing, ready-made flower presses are sold at craft stores and are fine for beginners.

## Method 3: The microwave (fastest, needs care)

This is the method for when you want results tonight. It works best with thin flowers, and it takes a bit of trial and error.

1. Place flowers between two sheets of baking paper or paper towel.
2. Put them between two microwave-safe ceramic plates, or under a microwave-safe dish, to weigh them down.
3. Microwave in short bursts of around 20 to 30 seconds, checking between each burst.
4. Let them cool and air out between bursts so they don't cook.

Stop as soon as they feel papery and dry. Overdo it and the colours fade or the petals turn brittle and brown. Never leave the microwave unattended while doing this. Colour retention is usually not quite as good as slow pressing, but for cards and quick projects it's very handy.

## Mistakes I've made so you don't have to

- **Pressing wet flowers.** They go mouldy or turn brown. Always dry the surface first.
- **Overlapping petals.** They stick together and tear when you separate them.
- **Peeking too early.** Lifting the paper every day disturbs the petals before they set. Leave them alone for at least a week with the slow methods.
- **Storing them in the open.** Pressed flowers fade in sunlight. Keep them between sheets of paper in a box or folder, out of the light.
- **Forgetting about humidity.** In a humid Queensland summer, everything takes longer to dry. Change the paper more often, and keep your press somewhere with good airflow.

## What to make with them

Once you've got a little collection, the fun starts:

- **Christmas cards and gift tags.** Glue small flowers to card stock with a tiny dab of PVA glue applied with a toothpick, then let it dry flat under a book.
- **Bookmarks.** Arrange flowers on a strip of card and cover with self-adhesive laminating sheets.
- **Framed botanicals.** A floating glass frame (two panes of glass) shows off flowers beautifully, front and back.
- **Phone cases and resin pieces.** These need extra steps and materials, so treat them as a second project once you're comfortable handling the flowers.

## Handling tips

Pressed flowers are fragile. Use tweezers to move them, and a soft paintbrush to nudge them into position. Work somewhere without a fan or open window, because one gust can scatter an afternoon's work across the floor. Ask me how I know.

## A good project for this weekend

If you're new to it, try this: pick six pansies or daisies on Saturday morning, press three in a heavy book and microwave the other three. Compare them in a fortnight. You'll quickly see which method suits your flowers, your patience and your plans, and you'll have the start of a collection in time for handmade Christmas cards.
